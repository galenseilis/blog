Kamloops 2026: Which Candidates Could Plausibly Improve Health?

An assessment of the 2026 BC election candidates in Kamloops Centre and Kamloops-North Thompson, focused on health policy, local health needs, and the plausibility of implementation.
BC Politics

Kamloops

Author

Galen Seilis

Published

October 22, 2026

The short version

  • Both Conservative incumbents, Peter Milobar (Kamloops Centre) and Ward Stamer (Kamloops-North Thompson), are heavily favoured to win again. 338Canada puts their odds at 99% and >99% respectively. They are the candidates most likely to be in a position to influence provincial health policy (338Canada: Kamloops Centre; Kamloops-North Thompson).
  • The NDP candidates have the most detailed primary-care plan and, in Kory Clark, the most directly relevant frontline health experience. But both NDP candidates are very unlikely to win these ridings.
  • The Greens, OneBC, and independents are not plausible government actors in either riding. Their role is agenda-setting and local advocacy, not implementation.
  • The local baseline is severe: about 1.26 million British Columbians are unattached to a family doctor; admitted patients at Royal Inland Hospital wait a 90th-percentile 34.5 hours in the emergency department; and Kamloops recorded 58 toxic-drug deaths in 2025.
  • On the specific question of who could actually move the needle, the central tradeoff is this: the candidates with the most access to power have the least detailed personal health plans; the candidates with the most detailed health plans have the least access to power.

This post assesses plausibility, not worthiness, and it is not a voting recommendation. The information is current to Oct. 22, 2026, two days before final voting.

What “improving health” would have to mean

Most of the campaign debate is about health care access, not population health. Those are related but not the same. A useful assessment separates four things:

  1. Primary-care attachment: whether people have a family doctor or nurse practitioner.
  2. Emergency and acute capacity: whether Royal Inland Hospital and rural ERs can see people in time.
  3. Toxic-drug deaths: whether the province can reduce the roughly five deaths per day.
  4. Workforce and capital: whether the province can recruit, retain, and train health workers and build facilities.

On the first, the benchmark is stark. In April 2026, Health Minister Josie Osborne said 1,259,425 people were not attached to a primary-care provider, while 77.8% of the population was attached (CBC, Sept. 25, 2026). The ministry says it is connecting about 4,000 people per week and that more than 600,000 have been connected since 2023. At 4,000 per week, clearing 1.26 million people would take roughly six years, before accounting for new unattachment. That is the scale of the promise.

On toxic drugs, BC recorded 2,315 deaths in 2024 and 1,826 in 2025. That was the first year below 2,000 since 2020, but it is still about five deaths per day. In 2026, monthly deaths have ranged from 109 to 150; July and August were the lowest for those months since 2019 (BC Coroners Service; province, Oct. 14, 2026).

In Kamloops, the city recorded 58 toxic-drug deaths in 2025, down from 91 in 2024. Earlier reporting used higher 2024 figures, and the numbers have been revised, so the direction is clearer than the exact decimal (Castanet, Aug. 31, 2026). Royal Inland Hospital sees about 82,000 emergency visits a year, or roughly 225 a day; for admitted patients, the 90th-percentile ED stay was 34.5 hours in 2024–25 (CIHI data via Kamloops Medicine).

Those are the outcomes any credible health claim should be judged against.

In 2024, Milobar won Kamloops Centre with 48.8% and a 2,003-vote margin over the NDP; Stamer won Kamloops-North Thompson with 59.7% and an 8,056-vote margin (bcballot). These are not swing-riding projections. They are strong-incumbent projections.

The Wren’s reader survey found that 79% of respondents chose health care as their top issue, which is a useful reminder that the local debate is not abstract (The Wren).

The candidates

Peter Milobar: Conservative, Kamloops Centre

Background. Milobar is the most experienced local politician on either ballot: two terms on Kamloops council, mayor from 2008 to 2017, five terms chairing the Thompson Regional Hospital District, and MLA since 2017. He first represented Kamloops-North Thompson and has represented Kamloops Centre since 2024 (Castanet profile; bcballot).

His 2026 party history is unusual and worth stating plainly: elected as a Conservative in 2024, he left the Conservative caucus on Aug. 14, 2026 and sat as an Independent; founded a new party with Ian Paton on Sept. 4; joined CentreBC as leader on Sept. 18; and rejoined the Conservatives on Sept. 22, the day the Assembly dissolved (bcballot). That does not tell you what he would do on health, but it is relevant to questions of team stability and negotiating effectiveness.

Health positions. Milobar’s campaign emphasizes repatriating BC-trained doctors working abroad, a $50-million rural emergency staffing fund, and local capital priorities: a PET/CT scanner for the cancer centre, a cardiac catheterization lab at Royal Inland Hospital, and completion of the cancer centre. He also supports involuntary care and “dry” supportive housing, and he wants to repeal DRIPA and add police and Crown counsel (Castanet; Conservative platform).

He says about 40% of Kamloops residents lack a family doctor, against roughly 20% provincially, and argues health funding has grown from $17 billion in 2017 to $38 billion (Castanet). He has previously called for accountability for ER closures and described health-care funding as adequate (The Wren).

Record. He was present for 94 of 105 divisions in the 43rd Parliament and voted for Bill M219, the Health Authorities Amendment Act requiring medically recommended wait times and public reporting. That motion was defeated 43–48 on Dec. 2, 2025. He also voted for Bill M235 (the Drug Recovery and Community Safety Act, which would cancel supervised-consumption funding and condition funding on abstinence), and against Bill 24 (vaping cost recovery) and Bill 32 (Mental Health Act amendment) (bcballot voting record).

Plausibility. High access, medium specificity. He is almost certain to be re-elected and likely to be in government, and he has more relevant local health governance experience than anyone else in these two ridings. Five terms on the hospital district is not nothing. His capital-project advocacy is concrete and locally legible. But his personal health agenda is thin on primary-care attachment and workforce retention, and the $50-million ER fund is modest against a health budget in the tens of billions. His party’s emphasis on involuntary care and ending safe supply is contested on the evidence and would not, by itself, address the 1.26-million unattachment problem. He also did not respond to The Wren’s health questionnaire.

Bottom line: the most plausible candidate to be in the room where health decisions are made, and a credible bet for capital and rural-staffing gains; a weaker bet for rapid primary-care attachment or a measurable reduction in toxic-drug deaths.

Ward Stamer: Conservative, Kamloops-North Thompson

Background. Stamer has spent about 45 years in forestry, starting at 14 in Blue River. He was a Barriere councillor and then mayor, acclaimed in 2022, and has been MLA for Kamloops-North Thompson since 2024. He is the Conservative shadow minister for forests and has deep roots in the rural parts of the riding (Castanet profile).

Health positions. His focus is rural ER staffing and removing regulatory friction: faster credential recognition for internationally trained health workers, spousal employment support so recruited clinicians will stay, a stronger locum system, more resources for rural emergency departments, and the party’s $50-million rural emergency fund. For Sun Peaks specifically, he has talked about replacing the community’s doctor, improving dedicated ambulance service especially on weekends, and expanding walk-in services where necessary. He claims a 95% success rate on constituent cases, including health issues, doctor connections, ICBC, and WorkSafeBC. That is a self-reported figure, but it speaks to the casework role of a rural MLA (Castanet; Sun Peaks Independent News).

In a 2024 interview with the Sun Peaks Independent News, Stamer said the Conservatives would spend more on health care and implement a patient-first funding model. He emphasized training more doctors locally, bringing home Canadians who studied medicine abroad, speeding up international credential recognition, reducing red tape, and incentivizing doctors and nurses to work in rural and remote communities. In February, he told Castanet the party would like to see cuts to administrative jobs in health care (The Wren).

Record. He was present for 103 of 105 divisions, voted for Bill M219 (wait-time accountability), for Bill M235 (drug recovery and community safety), against Bill 24 and Bill 32, and for Bills M204 (perinatal mental health), M214 (firefighters’ health), M213 (drug education), and M217 (dashboard cameras). He broke with his party once, on the Public Flags Display Act (bcballot voting record).

Plausibility. High access, medium specificity, high rural fit. Kamloops-North Thompson includes exactly the kinds of communities where ER closures have been most acute: Clearwater, Barriere, Chase, and the surrounding rural areas. His focus on locums, credential recognition, and spousal employment is directly relevant. He is also almost certain to be re-elected. But he has no personal costed health plan beyond the party platform, and his record is otherwise strongly party-line. His influence would likely be as a rural-riding advocate inside a Conservative government, not as a health policy designer.

Bottom line: a plausible rural-ER and credentialing advocate with real local government experience; less plausible as the author of a broad population-health strategy.

Mike Wisla: BC NDP, Kamloops Centre

Background. Wisla is 64, has lived in Kamloops for 17 years and the region for 37, and describes himself as “not a professional politician” (Castanet, Oct. 22). His campaign is pitched heavily at affordability: caps on grocery essentials and gas, removing the provincial fuel excise tax to save about 30 cents a litre, and freezing ICBC rates for three years.

Health positions. He largely carries the NDP platform: the claim that one million people have been connected to primary care; recruiting doctors from the US; the new SFU medical school; 1,000 new training seats; 23 mental-health and substance-use centres; involuntary care for severe addiction; and more shelter space (Castanet; BC NDP platform).

He did not respond to The Wren’s questionnaire. The Wren quoted a public statement in which he described his own experience in health care and said the province needs to recruit and retain more workers, make hospitals safer, and give people struggling with homelessness and addiction access to care (The Wren).

Plausibility. Low. He is a first-time candidate in a riding the NDP is projected to lose by a wide margin, and his personal health credentials are not evident from the public record. The NDP platform is the most detailed on primary-care attachment, but Wisla would be a backbencher in opposition, not a minister. His plausible role is to keep primary care and affordability on the local agenda.

Kory Clark: BC NDP, Kamloops-North Thompson

Background. Clark grew up in Cache Creek, has lived in Kamloops since 18, and spent seven years in health care, most recently as a community support worker with United Steelworkers Local 1-417. She is a member of the Michel Band and volunteers with Interior Health and the Bonaparte First Nation (Castanet).

Health positions. She combines the NDP platform with local specifics: tripling US recruitment, which she says has brought 800 health professionals to BC since March 2025; 20 new team-based primary-care clinics; 1,000 training seats; expanded rural loan forgiveness; and about $400 million invested in the region, including the cancer centre and a new urgent and primary care centre (The Wren; Castanet). In the Sun Peaks candidate questionnaire, she added that the NDP has added more than 1,800 specialists and purchased 21 MRI machines and 15 CT scanners since 2017, and that the party’s plan includes 8 new MRI machines and 10 new CT scanners, with priority for growing and rural communities. She also pointed to the completed tower at Royal Inland Hospital and the new cancer centre, and said she would work on a sustainable solution for Sun Peaks, which has been without a community health centre for a year (Sun Peaks Independent News).

Plausibility. Low access, high experiential credibility. Of all eight candidates, Clark has the most directly relevant frontline health experience and the clearest personal connection to the NDP’s primary-care plan. But she is running in a riding the NDP is very unlikely to win. If the criterion is who understands the health system from the inside, she scores well; if the criterion is who can implement, she does not have a realistic path in this election.

Annelise Beach: BC Green, Kamloops Centre

Background. Beach is a social worker with 10 years in social services. She moved to Kamloops from Burns Lake 13 years ago and is a first-time candidate (Castanet).

Health positions. The Greens propose a community health centre in every region, nurse recruitment and retention incentives, a review of loan forgiveness, and paid practicums for health students. Beach adds more primary care centres with GPs, nurse practitioners, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and mental-health support. She also supports a wealth tax, which she says would raise $22.2 billion in its first year (Castanet; The Wren; Green platform).

Plausibility. Very low in this riding: 13% and a distant third. The Green health plan is detailed on community-based care and mental health, and Beach’s social-work background fits it. But the Greens will not form government, and a single Green MLA in opposition would have limited direct influence. Her plausible contribution is agenda-setting: keeping community health centres, nurse retention, and paid practicums in the local debate.

Rocky McAfee: OneBC, Kamloops Centre

Background. McAfee is a long-time tattoo artist. He was the financial agent for a 2012 Kamloops-area committee that opposed the BC Liberals’ Harmonized Sales Tax, according to his OneBC candidate page. In an interview with CFJC Today, he identified health care, crime and public safety, and affordability as key issues, but did not specify how he would address health care (CFJC Today; OneBC; The Wren).

Health positions. The OneBC platform would allow private care alongside the public system, shift administrative and HR functions to the frontline, repeal the Health Professions and Occupations Act, end safe supply, and expand involuntary rehabilitation (OneBC platform).

Plausibility. Very low. He is polling around 4% in a riding with a 99%-favoured incumbent, and OneBC is not a realistic governing party. The private-care option is a major structural bet with uncertain effects on wait times and equity; it is not a detailed implementation plan.

Thaelo Proctor: Independent, Kamloops Centre

Background. Proctor is 23, a carpenter, and describes himself as working-class with no political experience. He is candid that he is a long shot (Castanet).

Health positions. He does not offer a detailed health plan. He says he could support Conservative, NDP, or Green health initiatives with stipulations, and that frontline workers should have a formal role in advocating for change and oversight. His broader pitch is to tax ultra-wealthy individuals and remove the bottom income-tax bracket (Castanet; The Wren).

Plausibility. Very low. An independent with no caucus and no health platform would have little ability to change provincial health policy, though he could press local issues from the outside.

Deborah Friesen: OneBC, Kamloops-North Thompson

Background. Friesen is a strata property manager in Kamloops; her professional profile lists Pacific Quorum (Okanagan) Properties. Her OneBC candidate page is mostly headings rather than a biography, and she did not respond to The Wren’s health questionnaire (OneBC; BCFSA; The Wren).

Health positions. She has more specific local health answers than McAfee, even though they come from a single questionnaire. For rural and small communities she proposes a stable clinic team, local blood and urine collection, basic diagnostic capacity, faster regional referrals, active screening outreach with follow-up, transport and staff housing, and hiring back health staff who were dismissed over vaccine mandates. The party platform adds a private option alongside the public system, administrative reform, repeal of the HPOA, ending safe supply, and involuntary rehabilitation (Sun Peaks Independent News; OneBC platform).

Plausibility. Very low. She is polling around 4% in a riding with a >99%-favoured incumbent, and she has no realistic path to government. Her rural-access list is concrete and directly relevant to the riding, but it is a list of service fixes rather than a costed plan, and OneBC is not a plausible governing party in this election.

The party plans, compared

The NDP has the most detailed supply and attachment plan: 20 new team-based primary-care clinics, a target of a same-day or 24-hour appointment through evening and weekend incentives, 1,000 training seats, rural loan forgiveness, 8 new MRI and 10 new CT scanners, 500 home-care workers, and a phase-out of agency nursing (bcballot health page; BC NDP platform). Its weakness is delivery: the 1.26-million unattachment figure shows how far the current trajectory is from universal attachment, and the plan does not fully explain how new clinics will be staffed when the province is already short of clinicians.

The Conservatives have 35 health commitments in a 405-commitment platform (bcballot). The main health-care commitments are a $50-million rural emergency fund, an expert commission within 100 days, a “Universal Health-Care Guarantee” that would fund treatment elsewhere if a patient exceeds a clinically recommended wait time, faster credential recognition, repatriation of Canadian-trained doctors abroad, seven long-term care homes ($1.43 billion), and a Mental Health Act amendment to enable mandatory treatment (bcballot health page). This is stronger on throughput, credentialing, and rural ER staffing than on primary-care attachment. The $50-million fund is small relative to the scale of the problem; the guarantee is a demand-side fix that depends on available capacity elsewhere; and the involuntary-care approach is contested.

The Greens have the most detailed community and prevention plan: a community health centre in every riding, safe nurse-to-patient ratios, a phase-out of agency nursing, six MSP-covered mental-health visits a year, single-entry surgical referrals, consolidation of health authorities, free vaccines, and loan forgiveness (bcballot health page; Green platform). It is the most coherent population-health package, but the Greens will not form government.

OneBC’s plan is the most structurally different: a private option alongside the public system, administrative devolution, repeal of the HPOA, and a punitive turn on drugs (OneBC platform). It is a high-variance bet, not a detailed implementation plan.

The bottom line

If “plausibility” means the chance of being in a position to implement health policy, the answer in these two ridings is Milobar and Stamer. They are the only candidates with a realistic path to government, and both have relevant local governance experience. Milobar’s edge is capital projects and hospital-district relationships; Stamer’s is rural ER staffing and constituency casework. Both voted for the M219 wait-time accountability motion.

If “plausibility” means the chance that their preferred health model would measurably improve health, the NDP and Green plans are more specific on primary-care attachment and community-based care, and Clark has the strongest frontline health background. But their candidates are very unlikely to be in government after Oct. 24.

That is the real tradeoff for a Kamloops voter who cares primarily about health: the candidates most likely to matter have the thinner health plans; the candidates with the stronger health plans are unlikely to matter in government. A reasonable reading is that Milobar and Stamer offer the best odds of incremental gains in capital, credentialing, and rural ER staffing, while the NDP and Green proposals offer the more serious diagnosis of the primary-care attachment crisis, but without a realistic path to power in these ridings.

Caveats

  • 338Canada’s numbers are probabilistic model outputs, not guarantees; riding polls and turnout can move them.
  • Party platforms are campaign documents; not every commitment is costed, and some are aspirational.
  • Overdose figures are revised over time; the Kamloops numbers here use the latest reporting (58 in 2025, 91 in 2024) and should be treated as provisional.
  • Candidate questionnaire non-responses are informative but not proof of indifference; campaigns are busy and some candidates rely on party platforms.
  • No all-candidates debates were listed for either riding on bcballot as of Oct. 22, 2026.
  • This is an assessment of plausibility, not a voting recommendation.
