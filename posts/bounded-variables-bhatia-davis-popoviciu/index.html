Bounding the Variance and the Mean: A Journey Through Bhatia-Davis and Popoviciu Inequalities

Mathematics
Statistics
Inequalities
Published

November 7, 2026

When analyzing a bounded random variable, understanding the interplay between its range, mean, and variance is crucial. In this post, we will formalize two classic statistical inequalities—Bhatia-Davis and Popoviciu—and explore a powerful, lesser-known application: inverting these variance bounds to strictly constrain an unknown mean.

1. Setup and Mathematical Foundations

Throughout this post, let \(X\) be a bounded real-valued random variable such that \(X \in [m, M]\) with a probability of \(1\).

  • \(m \in \mathbb{R}\) is the essential minimum value.
  • \(M \in \mathbb{R}\) is the essential maximum value (where \(m \leq M\)).
  • \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]\) is the expected value (mean).
  • \(\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^2]\) is the variance.

2. Proposition 1: The Bhatia-Davis Inequality

Proposition

Let \(X\) be a bounded random variable on \([m, M]\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Then: \[\sigma^2 \leq (M - \mu)(\mu - m)\]

Proof

Since \(X\) is strictly bounded between \(m\) and \(M\), the following inequality holds for all realizations of \(X\) with probability \(1\): \[(M - X)(X - m) \geq 0\]

Expanding the left side yields: \[MX - Mm - X^2 + mx \geq 0\] \[X^2 \leq (M + m)X - Mm\]

Taking the expectation (\(\mathbb{E}\)) on both sides due to the linearity of expectation: \[\mathbb{E}[X^2] \leq (M + m)\mathbb{E}[X] - Mm\] \[\mathbb{E}[X^2] \leq (M + m)\mu - Mm\]

By definition, variance can be expressed as \(\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2\). Substituting our upper bound for \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\) into this definition gives: \[\sigma^2 \leq (M + m)\mu - Mm - \mu^2\]

Factoring the right-hand side back into a product isolates the boundaries relative to the mean: \[\sigma^2 \leq M\mu + m\mu - Mm - \mu^2\] \[\sigma^2 \leq \mu(M - \mu) - m(M - \mu)\] \[\sigma^2 \leq (M - \mu)(\mu - m) \quad \blacksquare\]

3. Proposition 2: Popoviciu’s Inequality on Variance

Proposition

Let \(X\) be a bounded random variable on \([m, M]\) with variance \(\sigma^2\). Then: \[\sigma^2 \leq \frac{1}{4}(M - m)^2\]

Proof via the AM-GM Inequality

We can elegantly derive Popoviciu’s inequality by applying the Arithmetic Mean-Geometric Mean (AM-GM) inequality to the components of the Bhatia-Davis bound.

Let \(a = (M - \mu)\) and \(b = (\mu - m)\). Since \(m \leq \mu \leq M\), both \(a\) and \(b\) are non-negative real numbers. According to AM-GM (\(ab \leq (\frac{a+b}{2})^2\)): \[(M - \mu)(\mu - m) \leq \left( \frac{(M - \mu) + (\mu - m)}{2} \right)^2\]

Simplifying the numerator inside the parenthesis cancels out the mean \(\mu\): \[(M - \mu)(\mu - m) \leq \left( \frac{M - m}{2} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}(M - m)^2\]

Combining this with Proposition 1 completes the proof: \[\sigma^2 \leq (M - \mu)(\mu - m) \leq \frac{1}{4}(M - m)^2 \quad \blacksquare\]

Equality Condition

The Bhatia-Davis bound collapses to exactly match the Popoviciu upper bound if and only if \(a = b\), meaning: \[M - \mu = \mu - m \implies \mu = \frac{M + m}{2}\] Thus, Popoviciu’s inequality is a special, worst-case manifestation of Bhatia-Davis where the mean sits exactly at the interval’s midpoint.

4. Proposition 3: Bounding the Mean Given Variance

A highly practical problem emerges when the absolute limits \((m, M)\) and the variance \((\sigma^2)\) are known, but the true mean \((\mu)\) is hidden. We can invert the Bhatia-Davis quadratic structure to tightly trap the mean.

Proposition

Given a bounded random variable on \([m, M]\) with a known variance \(\sigma^2\), the true mean \(\mu\) must satisfy: \[\frac{M + m}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2} \leq \mu \leq \frac{M + m}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2}\]

Derivation

From Proposition 1, we start with the absolute constraint: \[\sigma^2 \leq (M - \mu)(\mu - m)\]

Expanding and gathering all terms onto the left side sets up a standard quadratic inequality in terms of \(\mu\): \[\sigma^2 \leq M\mu - Mm - \mu^2 + m\mu\] \[\mu^2 - (M + m)\mu + (Mm + \sigma^2) \leq 0\]

Next, we isolate \(\mu\) by completing the square for the first two terms. We add and subtract \(\left(\frac{M+m}{2}\right)^2\): \[\left(\mu - \frac{M+m}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{M+m}{2}\right)^2 + Mm + \sigma^2 \leq 0\]

Let’s simplify the constant fraction outside the parenthesis: \[-\left(\frac{M+m}{2}\right)^2 + Mm = -\frac{M^2 + 2Mm + m^2}{4} + \frac{4Mm}{4} = -\frac{M^2 - 2Mm + m^2}{4} = -\frac{(M - m)^2}{4}\]

Substituting this value back into the quadratic equation: \[\left(\mu - \frac{M+m}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{(M - m)^2}{4} + \sigma^2 \leq 0\]

Isolating the squared term: \[\left(\mu - \frac{M+m}{2}\right)^2 \leq \frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2\]

Taking the square root of both sides introduces an absolute value, defining the physical maximum distance the mean can stray from the midpoint: \[\left\vert \mu - \frac{M+m}{2} \right\vert \leq \sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2}\]

Expanding the absolute interval yields the final algebraic bounds: \[-\sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2} \leq \mu - \frac{M+m}{2} \leq \sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2}\] \[\mathbf{\frac{M + m}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2} \leq \mu \leq \frac{M + m}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{(M - m)^2}{4} - \sigma^2}} \quad \blacksquare\]

5. Conclusion

Without knowing the variance \(\sigma^2\), chaining the upper bounds of these equations yields a mathematical identity (\((M - \mu)(\mu - m) \leq \frac{1}{4}(M - m)^2\)) that offers no-tighter bounds on the mean. However, when variance is known, it helps refine the bounds.
